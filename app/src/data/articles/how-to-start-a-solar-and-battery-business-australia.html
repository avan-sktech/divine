The market you are entering

At the same time there are an estimated 5,046 solar installer businesses operating nationally in 2026, and that number is falling by about 7.3 per cent a year. Demand is surging while the operator base consolidates. The installers who build efficient sales systems now will take the share that leaves the market.

How to start a solar and battery business in Australia

The setup is a sequence of qualifications and registrations. Confirm the current detail with the official bodies named below, because requirements change, but the shape is consistent.

  • Register the business. Set up your structure, ABN and GST registration, and put professional indemnity and public liability insurance in place before you quote a single job.
  • Hold the electrical licence. Solar and battery installation is licensed electrical work. You need to be a licensed electrician or employ one, under your state or territory electrical safety regulator.
  • Get SAA accreditation. Accreditation now sits with Solar Accreditation Australia, not the Clean Energy Council, since early 2024. Only SAA-accredited installers are eligible under the Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme, which underpins the STC rebate. Always describe yourself as SAA-accredited and SRES-eligible.
  • Add battery accreditation. Battery work needs its own accreditation, and only about half of installers hold it, which is exactly why battery-ready installers are in demand right now.
  • Set up your rebate and compliance workflow. STC documentation, installation photos and checklists must be accurate. The Clean Energy Regulator now uses AI-assisted checking on battery installation photos, so clean documentation is not optional.

Do all of that and you are licensed, accredited and insured. You are also exactly where most installers stall, because being able to install is not the same as being able to sell.

The number that decides whether you survive: speed to lead

Here is the trap. Most new installers assume the constraint is lead volume, so they buy leads. Shared leads are sold to three to five competitors at once, cost $50 to $100 each, and convert at only 8 to 15 per cent. The real constraint is speed. The average installer takes 22 hours to respond to an enquiry, and booking rates fall from about 43 per cent when a lead is answered within minutes to roughly 2 per cent once a day has passed. Whoever answers first usually wins the job.

43% → 2%Solar appointment booking rates from a near-instant response versus a response after 24 hours. The industry average is 22 hours. This is the most fixable number in a new installer's business.

Trust is the second decider. 97 per cent of consumers read reviews before choosing a local trade business, 31 per cent will not use one rated under 4.5 stars, and about half of solar businesses sit on a 5-star rating built on only a handful of reviews. A brand-new business with no review base has to build proof deliberately, from the first job.
